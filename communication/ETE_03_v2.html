Module 1

Latency breakdown and delay mechanics

HIGH EXAM PRIORITY

Latency and its four components appear in calculation questions. Learn the formulas and the unit conversions in Module 2 together.

Definition

Latency is the total time required for an entire message to arrive at the receiver, measured from the moment the first bit is sent until the last bit arrives.

Total Delay (Latency) =
Transmission Time + Propagation Time + Processing Delay + Queuing Delay

Transmission Time (T.T)

Definition

The time required to push all bits of a message onto the physical transmission link.

Transmission Time = Message Size (bits) / Bandwidth (bps)

It depends on packet length and link capacity, not on physical distance.

Propagation Time (P.t)

Definition

The time required for a single bit (an electromagnetic or optical signal) to travel physically across the medium from sender to receiver.

Propagation Time = Distance (m) / Propagation Speed (m/s)

It depends on cable length and the speed of the medium.

Processing Delay

The time a router or switch takes to examine a packet's header, check for bit-level errors, and determine the outgoing port.

Queuing Delay, Buffering & Packet Loss

Queuing and buffering. When packets arrive faster than a router can process and transmit them, they wait in the router's memory buffer (a queue). The waiting time is the queuing delay.

Buffer overflow and packet loss. Router buffers have finite capacity. Sustained congestion fills the queue completely (buffer overflow), and the router must drop newly arriving packets, which is packet loss.

EXAM RULE

In data communications, 1 KB = 1000 bytes, not 1024. Always convert bytes to bits (× 8) before dividing by bandwidth in bps.

Worked Problem 1: Propagation vs. Transmission Time

Prompt: What are the propagation time and transmission time for a 2.5 KB message if the bandwidth of the network is 1 Gbps? Assume the distance between sender and receiver is 12,000 km and light travels at 2.4 × 108 m/s.

Step 1 (message size to bits)
2.5 KB = 2.5 × 1000 bytes = 2500 bytes
2500 × 8 bits = 20,000 bits
Step 2 (transmission time)
T.T = 20,000 bits / (1 × 109 bps)
= 0.00002 s = 20 µs
Step 3 (propagation time)
P.t = (12,000 × 1000 m) / (2.4 × 108 m/s)
= 0.05 s = 50 ms

Worked Problem 2: File Transfer Time Across Channels

Prompt: What is the time required to send a file of (i) 2 million bytes and (ii) 5 million bits using a channel with (i) 56 kbps and (ii) 1 Mbps?

(i) 2,000,000 bytes at 56 kbps
T.T = (2,000,000 × 8 bits) / (56 × 103 bps)
= 16,000,000 / 56,000 ≈ 285.71 s
(ii) 5,000,000 bits at 1 Mbps
T.T = 5,000,000 bits / (1 × 106 bps)
= 5 s
File size56 kbps1 Mbps
2 million bytes (16 million bits)285.71 s16 s
5 million bits89.29 s5 s
Full coverage of all four combinations. The two primary answers are the (2 million bytes, 56 kbps) and (5 million bits, 1 Mbps) cells.

Worked Problem 3: End-to-End Multi-Router Total Delay

Prompt: What is the total delay for a frame of 5 million bits sent on a link with 10 routers, each having a queuing time of 2 µs and a processing time of 1 µs? The link length is 2000 km, light speed inside the link is 2 × 108 m/s, and link bandwidth is 5 Mbps.

Step 1 (transmission time)
T.T = 5,000,000 bits / (5 × 106 bps) = 1 s
Step 2 (propagation time)
P.t = (2000 × 1000 m) / (2 × 108 m/s)
= 0.01 s = 10 ms
Step 3 (total queuing delay)
10 × 2 µs = 20 µs = 20 × 10-6 s
Step 4 (total processing delay)
10 × 1 µs = 10 µs = 10 × 10-6 s
Step 5 (total delay)
1 s + 0.01 s + 0.00002 s + 0.00001 s
= 1.01003 s

Module 3

Network reliability and security

Reliability metrics

Reliability and fault recovery are quantified with two time metrics that pull in opposite directions.

MetricDefinitionGoal
MTTR
Mean Time To Repair		The average time required to diagnose, fix, and restore a failed network component.Less time is better
MTBF
Mean Time Between Failure		The average operational time elapsed between one system failure and the next.More time is better

Security overview

Definition

Network security is the protection of data and network resources against unauthorized access, interception, and modification. Its three goals are Confidentiality (only authorized parties can read the data), Integrity (data is not altered), and Availability (the network and data are accessible when needed).

Module 4

Network connection types

Point-to-Point Connection · dedicated link

Provides a dedicated communication link between exactly two devices, reserving the entire channel capacity solely for their transmission.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Guarantees high speed, privacy, and zero channel contention.Scales poorly and costs more when linking many nodes directly.

Multipoint (Multidrop) Connection · shared link

Three or more devices share a single physical link, splitting channel capacity either spatially (simultaneous use) or temporally (time-shared turns).

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Reduces cabling cost and installation complexity.Introduces collisions, bandwidth sharing, and harder fault isolation.

Module 5

Network topologies

Definition

Network topology is the geometric arrangement (physical and logical layout) of links and nodes in a network. The five core topologies are Star, Bus, Mesh, Ring, and Hybrid.

Fault Detection Comparison

TopologyFault detectionWhy
StarEasyA broken cable affects only one node; the fault is isolated at that link.
BusDifficultFaults are hard to isolate, and one break in the backbone halts the whole network.
MeshVery easyEach link is dedicated point-to-point, so a faulty link is identified and isolated directly.
RingEasyCable faults are easy to identify, but a break or disabled node can stop a unidirectional ring.
HybridDepends on the partStar segments isolate faults well; the shared backbone is harder, as in a Bus.

Star Topology · central controller

Every end node connects through an individual point-to-point connection to a central controller (Switch or Hub).

Switch vs. Hub. A Switch inspects MAC (Media Access Control) addresses and forwards frames by Unicast to the exact destination. A Hub is a Layer 1 device that blindly Broadcasts signals to every port.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easy to install, simple fault detection (one broken cable affects only one node), and zero collisions when using a switch.The central device is a single point of failure, and it needs more cable than a bus.

Bus Topology · multipoint backbone

A multipoint configuration where all nodes connect to a main Backbone (bus) cable using vertical Drop Lines and Taps, with a Terminator (Resistor) at both ends of the backbone.

Signal flow. Transmissions broadcast along the backbone in both directions. Every node inspects the frame's MAC address and keeps the data only if the MAC matches its own.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Minimal cabling cost and simple initial setup.Difficult fault isolation, signal degradation over long backbones, and a single break in the backbone halts the entire network.

KEY QUESTION 1: WHY USE A RESISTOR IN THE TERMINATOR?

The terminator resistor absorbs the signal energy at the end of the cable so the signal does not bounce back (reflect) and interfere with new transmissions.

Key Question 2: How To Avoid Collisions?

Nodes use CSMA/CD (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection). A node listens to the bus before transmitting; if two nodes transmit together and a collision occurs, each waits a random backoff time before trying again.

Mesh Topology · fully connected

Every device has a dedicated point-to-point link to every other device. For n nodes this needs n(n-1)/2 duplex links.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Fault detection and isolation are very easy, privacy is high, and dedicated links eliminate traffic congestion.Massive cabling and I/O port requirements make it expensive and impractical for large networks.

Ring Topology · closed loop

Each device has a dedicated point-to-point connection only with the two devices on either side, forming a closed loop. Signals pass unidirectionally from node to node, often using token passing.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easy to identify cable faults, and access is orderly under high load.A single disabled node or break in a unidirectional ring can disable the entire network, unless a dual ring or bypass switch is used.

Hybrid Topology · two or more topologies

Integrates two or more different topologies, such as multiple Star networks connected together by a central Bus backbone.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Combines the fault isolation of Star with the scalability of a Bus backbone.Requires more complex configuration and costly backbone equipment.

The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) model divides network communication into seven layers, each with its own role. Data passes down the layers at the sender and up the layers at the receiver.
