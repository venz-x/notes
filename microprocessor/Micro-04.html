Module 1

Logic levels, buffers and tri-state bus isolation

Definitions

Logic 0 / 1: two voltage ranges the chip treats as the numbers 0 and 1. Buffer: a gate that copies its input to its output. Tri-state buffer: a buffer with a third state, “off”. Enable (E): the control wire that turns the buffer on or off. High-Z (high impedance): the output is disconnected, as if the wire were cut. Bus: wires shared by several chips. C, A, D: the Control bus (read/write and timing signals), the Address bus (the location wanted, MP to memory) and the Data bus (the value itself, 16-bit, both directions).

Voltage levels

On a 5 V system, 0 V to 1 V counts as logic 0 and 4 V to 5 V counts as logic 1. The middle band (1 V to 4 V) is undefined. High-Z is not a voltage level, it means the output is simply not connected.

Bus isolation

Two memories cannot both push data onto the same wire: if one sends 0 and the other 1, the wire is shorted. Each memory output goes through a tri-state buffer. Only the selected memory has E = 1 and drives the bus. The other goes to High-Z, so electrically it is not there. This lets many memory chips share one 16-bit data bus.

A note on the arrows: a triangle points in the direction the signal flows through it. On the Read line the signal goes from the MP toward the memory, so the triangle points at the memory. Data coming back to the MP on D would need a tri-state buffer pointing the other way, at the bus.

Module 2

8086 address space, hex and little-endian storage

Definitions

Byte-addressable: every address holds one byte (8 bits). Fetch: bringing instructions or data from memory into the microprocessor. Hexadecimal (H): base 16, using 0–9 and A–F; a short way to write binary. Word: 16 bits, two bytes. HB / LB: higher and lower byte of a word. Little-endian: the small end (lower byte) goes first, at the lower address.

My question: three hex questions

1. What is H? The suffix H means the number is hexadecimal (base 16), so 1234H is not decimal 1234.

2. Why is each hex digit 4 bits? One digit has 16 values (0 to 15) and 24 = 16. So 12H = 0001 0010 (8 bits, 1 byte) and 1234H = 0001 0010 0011 0100 (16 bits, 2 bytes).

3. Why is FFFFFH 20 bits? It has 5 digits, 5 × 4 = 20 bits: FFFFFH = 1111 1111 1111 1111 1111 and 00000H = 0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 (A19 to A0).

Word splitting and little-endian rule

The word 1234H splits into the higher byte HB = 12H and the lower byte LB = 34H. Intel stores the lower byte at the lower address and the higher byte at the higher address.

Module 3

The 16-bit serial bottleneck and memory banking

Definitions

Read cycle: one trip on the bus to get data from memory. Serial access: doing things one after another. Memory bank: a separate memory chip group that can be used at the same time as another. Even / Odd bank: the bank for even addresses (0, 2, 4…) and the bank for odd addresses (1, 3, 5…).

The serial bottleneck

The 8086 data bus is 16 bits wide, but a single linear memory gives only 8 bits per address. To read the word 1234H it must read address 0 (34H), then address 1 (12H): two read cycles for one word.

The banking solution

Split the 1 MB space into two 512 KB banks. The Even Bank holds addresses 0, 2, 4… and the Odd Bank holds 1, 3, 5…. A word is always one byte in each bank, so both are read at the same time and the 8086 gets 16 bits in one cycle.

Module 4

8086 even and odd memory banks

Definitions

Chip select (CS): a pin that switches a chip on or off. Active-low: the chip is on when the signal is 0 (“if 0 then on”) and off when it is 1. Row: the location inside a bank chosen by the address lines. Lower / higher bank: the bank on the low data lines (D7–D0) and on the high data lines (D15–D8).

Each bank is 512 KB = 219 bytes, so it needs 19 address lines. A19 down to A1 (19 lines) are wired in parallel to both banks and pick the row. A0 is not sent to the banks as an address: it is used only to choose between them.

Why Even = D7–D0 and Odd = D15–D8?

It follows from little-endian storage. In a word, the lower byte (LB) is at the even address and the higher byte (HB) is at the odd address. On the 16-bit data bus, the lower byte of a word belongs on the lower half (D7–D0) and the higher byte on the upper half (D15–D8). So the Even bank is wired to D7–D0 and the Odd bank to D15–D8, and every word lands in the right place with no swapping. It is a design convention, not a law of physics: it also means an 8-bit access at an even address always uses the low half of the bus.

Recombining the data bus

The Even bank drives D7–D0 (34H) and the Odd bank drives D15–D8 (12H). Together they form 1234H in a single cycle.

Module 5

Bank selection (BHE and A0) and aligned access

Definitions

BHE (Bus High Enable): an 8086 output pin, active-low, that turns on the Odd bank. A0: the lowest address bit; 0 for even addresses, 1 for odd. Aligned word: a 16-bit word that starts at an even address. Misaligned word: a word that starts at an odd address.

A0 is the active-low chip select of the Even bank (A0 = 0 turns it on). The 8086 pin BHE is the active-low chip select of the Odd bank (BHE = 0 turns it on).

Aligned vs misaligned words

A word at an even address (0–1, 2–3) has both bytes in the same row, with the same A19–A1, so one cycle fetches it. A word at an odd address (1–2) has its two bytes in different rows (different A19–A1), so the 8086 needs two bus cycles.
